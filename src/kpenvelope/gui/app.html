2. Results

Fixed charge at each boundary between layers.

Each boundary between two layers holds a fixed electric charge. It is set by the two materials, by how much they are stretched, and by any defect lines. A positive charge pulls electrons to the boundary (this is what makes the electron layer of a GaN transistor). A negative one pulls holes.

Where the free electrons collect, and how many there are.

The app works out where the free electrons settle. Near the surface it uses quantum mechanics (exact); deeper down a simpler model is enough.

How easily the electrons move (mobility) ?

How sure can you be of the electron count?

No input is known perfectly. Enter how far off each one could be (one standard deviation, the usual "plus or minus"). Leave a box at 0 if you know that number exactly.

What a measurement with a metal contact on top would show.

The app puts a metal contact on top and steps its voltage. Capacitance (C-V) shows how the electron layer empties as the voltage goes negative, which tells you where a transistor switches off. Current (I-V) shows how much current flows from top to bottom.

Gate leakage counts electrons that pass over the energy step (thermionic emission) and through the barrier (tunneling), using the bands of the C-V model. It assumes an ideal barrier: real gates often leak more, through defects. Below the switch-off voltage the model's bands stop changing, so trust it above that voltage. No transport numbers are needed.

C-V needs none of the numbers below. The electrons stay connected to the side contacts and only the gate voltage changes. Holes are left out, because a quick sweep cannot create them in these materials.

Layer stack
The layers grown on top of each other, listed from the top down here, with the bottom layer as the thick base (buffer).
Fraction
How much of the metal in an alloy is the named one. AlGaN with 0.25 means 25 percent aluminum, 75 percent gallium.
Relaxed
A thin layer on a different base is squeezed or stretched to fit it. Above some thickness it relaxes back toward its own size by forming dislocation lines. 0 percent means it fits the layer below exactly.
Interface charge
A fixed charge at the boundary between two layers. In nitrides it comes from polarization: the crystals are electrically polar, and the polarity changes from one layer to the next.
Electron sheet density
How many free electrons sit in the thin layer at an interface, per square centimeter. Transistors use these electrons.
Surface energy step (surface barrier)
How much energy an electron would need at the top surface. It depends on the surface and on any metal there.
Energy an electron needs (conduction band)
The blue line in the Electrons plot. Where it dips below the filling level, electrons collect.
Filling level (Fermi level)
States below it are filled with electrons, states above it are empty. The plots put it at 0.
Metal gate and plain contact (Schottky and ohmic)
A metal gate has an energy step that blocks electrons, so its voltage controls them. A plain contact lets electrons pass freely.
Mobility
How easily a carrier moves when pushed by a voltage. Higher means more current.
Holes
Missing electrons, which act like positive carriers.
Error bar
The range the answer probably lies in (one standard deviation), given how well the inputs are known.
Capacitance-voltage (C-V)
A standard measurement: as the gate voltage goes more negative, electrons leave, and the capacitance drops. The voltage where it drops is where the transistor switches off (the threshold).
Saturation speed
The fastest electrons can drift when pushed hard. It limits the current of a short transistor.
Self-heating
The current warms the transistor. Warm electrons move less easily, so the current drops.
Traps and drain lag
Defects that hold electrons for a while. After a high voltage they stay filled, and the current is low until they empty.
fT and fmax
How fast a transistor can work. Above fT it no longer amplifies current, above fmax it no longer amplifies power.
Breakdown
At a high enough voltage, fast carriers knock new electrons loose, which knock out more, and the current runs away.
Hot electrons
In a strong field electrons gain energy faster than they can pass it to the crystal, so they get much hotter than the crystal itself.
Passivation and field plate
A thin insulating film over the surface, and a metal strip on it joined to the gate that spreads out the strong field next to the gate.
Thermal runaway
When a device's current grows as it warms (leakage does), more heat makes more current makes more heat, until no steady temperature is left.
Peltier and Thomson heat
Carriers carry heat with them. Where they cross a contact or a junction, it warms or cools (Peltier); where the current runs along a temperature difference, a little heat is added or taken (Thomson).
Band-to-band tunneling and tunnel diodes
In a heavily doped junction the bands bend so steeply that electrons can tunnel from the valence band on one side straight into the conduction band on the other. A tunnel (Esaki) diode uses this: its forward current rises, falls and rises again.
Process steps
How a device is made: films put down (deposition), material removed (etching), oxide grown, dopants shot in (implant) and spread by heating (anneal).
Quantum states in the channel
In a layer only a few nanometers thick, electrons behave as waves: they can only take certain energies, and they sit a little away from the wall that holds them. The Transistor tab can include this.
What this app does not do
The app builds transistors from boxes. The Python package also does curved shapes on triangle meshes, tunneling through barriers inside the device, and devices made from the process steps; see the documentation for those and for the limits.

Every built-in number names its source; see the "Charges" tab, and the documentation of kpenvelope.
